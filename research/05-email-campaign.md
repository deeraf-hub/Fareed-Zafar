## Read this before the emails

I checked every company on your list against one question: **if you email them, is there anyone at the other end who can actually say yes?**

The answer for most of them is no, and the reason matters.

| What I found | Count | How solid is it |
|---|---:|---|
| Cold email can realistically work | **16** | Evidence based |
| Procurement gated. Register as a supplier, do not pitch | **8** | Evidence based |

So: **44 companies have a confident answer** (16 yes, 28 no). **8 have a real but slow door.** The remaining **53 are homework**, and I would rather hand you an honest gap than a confident guess.

**The pattern across all four researched sectors is the same, and it is not close.**

- **OpenAI has a full-time Presentation Designer role posted inside its Creative Studio.** Vanta's Brand Designer spec lists "presentation decks" as a core deliverable. Snowflake, Splunk and CrowdStrike all name "presentation templates" or "keynote presentations" in live job specs. These companies are hiring salaried people to do the exact thing you would be pitching.
- **Salesforce is triple-covered.** In-house Global Brand Studio, GPJ as agency of record for Dreamforce, and overflow already going to Aquent Studios and Superside.
- **McKinsey runs Visual Graphics and Media across 130+ offices.** Bain runs Creative Services with offshore design pods in Kuala Lumpur and Mexico. Deloitte runs the Green Dot Agency in-house. Presentation design is not a service these firms buy. It is the product they sell.
- **Houlihan Lokey runs a Presentations and Graphics department and a London Presentations Studio.** J.P. Morgan staffs Presentations Specialists in Mumbai. Goldman runs an Analytics and Exhibit Group in Bengaluru.
- **Chime's CMO has publicly said they no longer keep a creative agency on retainer** and expect AI to save millions in agency costs.

Your own PDF said the same thing in gentler words: *"the list should be treated as a lead-research draft, not a ready-to-contact prospect list."* It was right.

**So here is what this document does.** It gives you 16 real emails for the 16 companies where a human can say yes. It gives you a completely different approach for the 30 that are procurement gated, because pitching them is wasted effort but registering with them is not. It tells you to skip the 29, and where their work actually goes instead. And it is honest about the 30 I could not check.

**One more thing, and it is the most useful sentence here.** Of everything I found, the strongest evidence of a company that genuinely buys freelance design was Gusto, whose in-house Brand Studio is documented as tapping its network of creatives *once or twice a month*, and PagerDuty, whose design team was built as five people of whom **three were contract designers**. ## About "find their emails"

I found every email address that is actually published on the open web. They are in the tables below and they are real.

**What I did not do is guess.** Nobody publishes `firstname.lastname@` for a Director of Brand Design, and constructing one is worse than having nothing: it bounces, and enough bounces will get your domain classified as a spam source. That damage takes months to undo and it is not worth a lucky guess.

So the addresses split three ways.

**Press mailboxes.** `press@ramp.com`, `press@drata.com` and the rest are genuine, but they route to communications people. Zapier's press page says outright it only responds to members of the press. Deel's says it cannot handle non-press requests. A design pitch to a press inbox is a delete. I have marked which ones have named humans behind them, because those are worth a polite ask for a forward.

**Supplier and procurement mailboxes.** These are the real find. `Prospective_Suppliers@crowdstrike.com`, `procurement@snowflake.com`, `procurement@datadoghq.com`, `jpmorgan.supplier.setup@jpmorgan.com`, `BX-VendorOnboarding@Blackstone.com`, `BCGProcPaySupport@bcg.com`, `EYInclusiveProcurement@eyg.ey.com`. These exist precisely so vendors can get in. Different message, longer game, real door.

**Named individuals with published direct addresses.** Rare and valuable. Warburg Pincus publishes Kerrie Cohen, Managing Director and Global Head of Communications and Marketing, at `kerrie.cohen@warburgpincus.com`. William Blair publishes Padraic Swanton at `pswanton@williamblair.com`. Lazard publishes two. These people are on the record and reachable.

For everyone else, the route is: find the named person on LinkedIn, then get the address through a finder tool, then verify it before you send. Section at the end covers that.

## The supplier registration email

This is not a pitch and must not read like one. Short, factual, boring on purpose. Procurement teams are not persuaded, they are processed.

**Subject:** supplier registration, presentation and graphic design

> Hello,
>
> I would like to register as a supplier for presentation design, sales collateral and data visualisation.
>
> Details for your records:
>
> Trading name: [your registered name]
> Registered address: [address]
> Services: presentation and pitch deck design, sales enablement collateral, one-pagers, brochures, data visualisation, report and document design
> Company or tax registration number: [number]
> Insurance: [professional indemnity, if you hold it]
> Portfolio: [link]
>
> If there is a form or portal you would rather I use, please point me at it and I will complete it.
>
> Thank you,
> Fareed [surname]
> [phone] · [email]

**Two practical notes.** Get professional indemnity insurance before you send these, because most enterprise onboarding asks for it and having none stops the process at step one. And expect no reply. Registration is a record, not a conversation.

# How to actually get the addresses

For most of Tier A the target is a named person with no published address. The honest workflow is four steps and the fourth is not optional.

1. **Find the human on LinkedIn.** Company plus the title I gave you. This takes seconds and costs nothing.
2. **Get a candidate address** from a finder tool. Apollo, Hunter, RocketReach, Lusha, Prospeo and FindyMail all do this, most with a small free tier that is enough for sixteen lookups.
3. **Verify it before you send.** NeverBounce, ZeroBounce, Bouncer or MillionVerifier. A verifier tells you whether the mailbox exists without sending anything to it.
4. **Bin anything that comes back risky or catch-all.** A guess that "probably works" is the thing that gets your domain flagged.

**I could not verify current pricing for any of these tools**, because the session ran out of web search budget. Check before you buy, and start on free tiers regardless. Sixteen contacts does not justify a subscription.

## Do not send from your main domain

If you take one operational thing from this document, take this.

Buy a second domain that resembles your main one. Set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC on it. Warm it slowly, starting at five to ten emails a day and building over a few weeks. Send cold outreach only from that one.

The reason is simple. If cold sending damages a domain's reputation, and sometimes it will regardless of how careful you are, you want the damage contained on a domain you can abandon rather than on the address your actual clients use to reach you.

**Sixteen emails is a tiny campaign.** You do not need automation software. Send them by hand, one at a time, personalised properly. Sequencing tools exist to solve a problem you do not have, and they introduce the tracking problem below.

# The legal part, which genuinely matters here

**Caveat first, because it is important.** The research on this could not be verified against primary sources in this session, for the same search budget reason. The instrument names and section numbers are given so you can check each point, and two figures specifically need confirming before you rely on them. Treat this as a well-informed checklist, not legal advice.

## The three rules that actually apply to your list

**1. Turn off open tracking and click tracking for every UK and EU recipient.**
Tracking pixels and rewritten links fall under PECR regulation 6 and ePrivacy Article 5(3). These are consent rules and, unlike the corporate subscriber exemption, they have no business carve-out. A cold recipient has by definition given no consent. Most cold email platforms turn open tracking on by default, which makes this the single most commonly broken rule in the field, and it is one checkbox.

**2. Some EU countries require prior consent even for business email.**
Germany, Austria, Italy, Spain, Denmark, the Netherlands, Belgium, Greece and Poland. Legitimate interest does not override this, because ePrivacy takes precedence over GDPR here.

**On your list this bites immediately: Dynatrace is Austrian.** Capgemini is French, which is opt-out but only where the offer genuinely relates to the recipient's own job. Savills, Knight Frank, Amnesty and Oxfam are UK. MSF and the World Economic Forum are Swiss.

**Segment your list by country before the first send.** Never run one sequence across all of them, because the rules are mutually incompatible.

**3. Every email needs a real footer.**
Your real name, a genuine physical postal address, a working reply address, a one-step unsubscribe, a line saying it is a marketing message, one line saying where you got their address, and a link to a privacy notice. For UK and EU recipients, state the right to object separately from the rest of the boilerplate rather than buried in it.

Yes, this makes the email look less like a personal note. Send it anyway. The alternative is exposure you cannot afford as a sole trader.

## Two things never to do

**Never fake a "Re:" or a reply thread.** No invented prior conversation, no "as discussed", no forwarded-looking subject line. It breaches CAN-SPAM, US state law with per-email damages, ePrivacy and PECR simultaneously. There is no jurisdiction where it is legal, and it is the fastest way to turn a marketing problem into a legal one.

**Never keep emailing someone who said no.** Any negative reply is an opt-out, including "not interested" and an auto-reply refusing solicitation. Stop on receipt, not within the statutory window. Keep a permanent suppression list.

## Two numbers to confirm before you rely on them
The current CAN-SPAM per-email civil penalty (the last confirmable figure was $53,088 for 2025, adjusted each January), and whether the UK's Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 has commenced the PECR penalty rise from £500,000 to UK GDPR levels. Neither could be checked live.
