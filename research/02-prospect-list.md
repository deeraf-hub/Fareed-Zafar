# Segment A — Active hiring signal (14)

**Why these are first.** Each of these organisations posted a job for someone to make presentations, proposals or pursuit graphics. That means the need is proven, the budget line exists, and nobody has to be convinced the work is worth paying for. You are only competing on *who*, not *whether*.

**The angle.** Do **not** position yourself as an alternative to the hire — the hiring manager owns that requisition and will read it as a threat. Position as **overflow and peak-week capacity**: "you clearly have more deck work than one person can absorb, and hiring takes 3 months." If the role has since been filled, the angle gets *stronger*, not weaker: the new hire is drowning in month one.

# Segment B — Agencies & studios for white-label work (21)

**Why this is the highest-ROI segment on the entire list.** These businesses sell decks. Their constraint is production capacity, not demand. They already have a process for briefing external designers, they already have clients, and they pay without you needing to educate anyone. **One agency that adds you to its bench replaces months of cold outreach.**

**The angle.** You are not a competitor and you must not sound like one. Lead with **capacity, reliability and brand-discipline** — your ability to work inside *their* templates, *their* client's brand, and *their* deadlines, invisibly. Never pitch narrative or strategy: that is the margin they sell, and offering it makes you a threat instead of a resource.

**How to price them.** Per-slide or per-deck, never hourly. They resell your time and need clean margin maths.

# Segment C — Recently funded startups (31)

**The critical nuance — get this right or this segment will not reply.** A company that *just closed a round* does **not** need a pitch deck. They finished that job. What breaks immediately after a raise is everything downstream of it: the sales deck is still the fundraising deck with the ask slide deleted, there's no one-pager for the new enterprise leads, and there's suddenly a board that expects a monthly update deck with real data in it.

**So sell:** sales decks · one-pagers and leave-behinds · investor-update templates · board decks · conference and booth collateral · data visualisation for the metrics they now have to report.
**Don't sell:** "a better pitch deck." Save that for the 12–18-month re-trigger (the pre-seed and seed companies here will be raising again inside that window — diary every single one).

**Timing.** Contact within 6 weeks of the announcement. After ~3 months the moment has passed and you're cold again.

**Who to contact.** At pre-seed/seed, message the **founder/CEO directly** — they still make every spend decision. At Series A and above, go to **Head of Marketing / Head of Growth / Chief of Staff**; the founder has already stopped reading DMs.

# Segment D — VCs, accelerators & fund managers (10)

**Why this segment is worth more than its size.** Two revenue lines from one contact. First, the firm itself needs decks — LP fundraising decks, annual LP updates, demo-day templates, portfolio-review packs. Second, and much bigger: **a pre-seed partner speaks to dozens of founders a month, and every one of them needs a deck.** Becoming the designer a fund recommends is the closest thing to an annuity in this business.

**The angle.** Never ask for a referral in message one — it reads as extractive and gets ignored. Lead by *giving*: offer a free teardown of one portfolio company's public deck. It's useful to them whether or not they hire you, and it demonstrates judgement rather than claiming it.

**A gap worth exploiting:** emerging managers raising **Fund I** have exactly the same problem as a first-time founder — they must raise from LPs using a deck, and they usually have no design resource at all. Only **177 emerging-manager funds closed** in the year to 2026 (the lowest since 2015) and the median US VC fund fell to **$15.3M** in Q1 2026 — meaning a large number of managers are still out there right now, raising, with a deck that isn't working.

# Segment E — Events, conferences & associations (10)

**Why these are reliable.** Every one of these organisations publishes a **sponsorship prospectus and exhibitor brochure every year**, without fail, because that document is how the event gets funded. It's recurring, calendar-driven and already budgeted. Win it once and it repeats annually.

**Timing is everything.** The 2027 prospectus gets commissioned roughly **9–12 months before the event** — which for most of these means **now through Q4 2026**. Never pitch in the six weeks before an event; the whole team is in operations mode and nothing else gets read.

**Also sell the back end:** the post-event impact report and attendee-data recap deck sent to sponsors to justify renewal. Almost nobody pitches this, and it's the document that decides whether sponsors come back next year.

# Segment G — Nonprofits & foundations (5)

**The trigger is the calendar, and it's a reliable one.** Annual and impact reports run on a fixed cycle, and the sector has a widely-acknowledged quality problem: **nearly four in five nonprofits use an impact or annual report to support fundraising, but only 16% say those reports are very effective.** That gap is your entire pitch, and you can quote it verbatim.

**What sells:** annual report · impact report · case for support (the document behind every major-gift conversation) · donor and board decks · programme data visualisation · flipbook and digital report layout.

**Pricing warning.** Nonprofit graphic design averages **$26.90/hr** against $42.86/hr in the wider market. Don't compete at that rate — pitch **foundations and large national organisations** rather than small local ones, and scope by project, never hourly.
