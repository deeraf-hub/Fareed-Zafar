# Outreach Playbook

How to work the list so the reply you get is the one you expect.

---

## 1. The core principle

Every prospect on this list is there because of a **dated, public, verifiable event** — a job posting, a funding round, a dropped sponsorship, a published prospectus. That event is your first line. It does three things at once: it proves you did homework, it explains why you're writing *today*, and it makes the message impossible to confuse with the fifty other "I design pitch decks" messages they got this week.

**Never open with what you do. Open with what happened to them.**

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| "Hi, I'm a presentation designer with 6 years of experience…" | "Saw you posted a Presentation Designer role in Boston on the 12th — four days on-site is a tough ask for that skillset." |
| "I can help you with your pitch deck." | "Congrats on the $8M from Square Peg. The first thing that usually breaks after a seed is the sales deck." |
| "Do you outsource design work?" | "You've got 5,000 decks shipped and a San Diego studio — do you keep a freelance bench for overflow weeks?" |

---

## 2. Work the segments in this order

Effort follows conversion. Do not start at prospect #1 and grind to #100.

### Week 1–2 → **Segment B (agencies)** and **Segment A (hiring signal)**
These are the two segments where nobody has to be sold on the idea that presentation design is worth paying for. Segment B especially: an agency that puts you on its freelance bench sends you work for years without you ever writing another cold message. **This is the single highest-ROI block on the list.** Contact all 20 in Segment B in the first fortnight.

### Week 3–4 → **Segment D (VCs & accelerators)**
Low volume, enormous leverage. A partner at a pre-seed fund talks to 30 founders a month, every one of whom needs a deck. You are not selling the fund a deck — you are asking to be the designer they recommend. Offer them something free and useful (a deck teardown for one portfolio company) to earn the referral.

### Week 5–8 → **Segment C (funded startups)**
High volume, medium conversion, but the fastest cash. Time-sensitive: hit them inside 6 weeks of the announcement.

### Ongoing / seasonal → **Segments E, F, G**
Calendar-driven. Conferences buy their prospectus design 9–12 months before the event. Nonprofits commission annual reports at fiscal-year end. Sports properties buy sponsorship decks before the season sales cycle. Diary these rather than blitzing them.

---

## 3. What each segment actually buys

Match the offer to the segment or the reply rate collapses.

| Segment | What they need | What NOT to lead with |
|---|---|---|
| **A — Hiring signal** | Overflow capacity, peak-week support, template systems, "the work the new hire can't get to yet" | Don't pitch as a replacement for the role — you'll get ignored by the hiring manager |
| **B — Agencies** | White-label production capacity, fast turnaround, your ability to work in *their* brand and *their* process | Don't pitch strategy or narrative — that's their margin, you're threatening it |
| **C — Funded startups** | Sales deck, one-pager, investor update template, conference collateral, data viz for the metrics they now have to report | Don't pitch a *pitch deck* — they just finished raising. Their deck problem is solved for 12 months |
| **D — VC / accelerators** | LP decks (for the fund itself), portfolio deck support, demo-day templates | Don't ask for a referral in message one |
| **E — Events / associations** | Sponsorship prospectus, exhibitor brochure, floorplan graphics, post-event impact report | Don't pitch in the 6 weeks before their event — nobody is listening |
| **F — Sports / esports** | Sponsorship sales decks, partnership one-pagers, audience/data viz, renewal recap decks | Don't pitch generic "brand design" — they buy *revenue* collateral |
| **G — Nonprofits / foundations** | Annual report, impact report, case for support, donor and board decks, data viz | Don't lead on price — lead on donor trust and clarity |

**Note the single most important nuance:** a company that *just closed a round* does **not** want a pitch deck. They want the collateral that turns money into revenue — sales decks, one-pagers, data visualisation. Pitch-deck work comes from companies **12–18 months post-raise** or **currently raising**. Get this backwards and Segment C will not reply.

## 4. Message templates

### 4.1 LinkedIn connection note (max 300 characters — this is the hard constraint)

Keep it to: trigger + one specific observation + no ask.

> **Segment A**
> "Saw the Presentation Designer opening in Boston. Pursuit decks under an RFP deadline are a different animal from marketing slides — happy to be a backstop for peak weeks while you hire. No pitch, just leaving it here."

> **Segment B**
> "5,000+ decks and a San Diego studio — that's a serious throughput. Do you run a freelance bench for overflow? I do production-grade PowerPoint in someone else's brand system without needing hand-holding."

> **Segment C**
> "Congrats on the seed. Usually the week after a raise is when someone notices the sales deck is still the fundraising deck with the ask slide deleted. If that's on your list, I'm around."

> **Segment D**
> "Pre-seed founders lose more deals to a confusing deck than a weak business. I'd happily do a free teardown on one portfolio deck — useful to you either way, no strings."

> **Segment F**
> "Noticed you're out of the 2026 partner programme. Rebuilding a sponsorship story for non-endemic brands is exactly the deck problem I like. Worth a look at your current sales deck?"

### 4.2 Email — first touch

Four sentences. No attachments. One link.

```
Subject: <specific trigger, no adjectives>

Hi <name>,

<Trigger — one sentence, dated, factual.>

<One sentence of insight that shows you understand their situation,
not your service.>

<What you'd do — concrete, scoped, small.>

Portfolio: <link>. Worth 15 minutes?

<name>
```

**Worked example — Segment C:**

```
Subject: Congrats on the $8M — one thought on what comes next

Hi Luke,

Saw Reservoir closed $8M on the 12th, led by Asymmetric.

The Boston-metro expansion means your install crews and utility
partners will need something to leave behind, and a homeowner-facing
one-pager is a very different document from the deck that raised the
round.

I'd start with two things: a partner/utility deck and a single-page
installer leave-behind, in your existing brand. Fixed price, about a
week.

Portfolio: <link>. Worth 15 minutes?

Fareed
```

### 4.3 Follow-up cadence

| Touch | Day | Channel | Content |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 | LinkedIn connect + note | Trigger, no ask |
| 2 | +3 | Email | Full first touch (above) |
| 3 | +8 | LinkedIn DM (if connected) | One piece of *specific* value — e.g. a redesigned slide from their public deck |
| 4 | +17 | Email | One line: "Still worth a look, or should I close the loop?" |
| 5 | +45 | Email | New trigger only. If there's no new trigger, don't send. |

**Stop at 5.** Then move them to a quarterly re-trigger list.

## 5. The move that converts best

For Segments C, F and G, **do the work first, uninvited, and small.**

Find one slide of theirs that's publicly available — a deck on their site, a conference talk, a sponsorship page, an annual report spread — rebuild **one** slide, and send it as an image in message 3. Not a full deck. One slide.

This costs you 25 minutes and it converts several times better than any written pitch, because it moves the conversation from "can you design?" to "can you do this for the other 19 slides?" — which is a scoping question, not a trust question.

## 6. Pricing anchors (from market data gathered during this research)

Use these to sanity-check what you quote — you are competing against these numbers, not against nothing.

| Benchmark | Rate | Source context |
|---|---|---|
| US in-house presentation designer | **$42.86/hr average** (range $31.97–$45.43) | ZipRecruiter, Aug 2026 |
| Suffolk, salaried Presentation Designer, Boston | **$78,000–$106,000/yr** | Job posting, Aug 2026 |
| Allied Sports, Presentation & Brand Designer, NYC | **$75,000–$85,000/yr** | Job posting, Aug 2026 |
| Freelance presentation designer, open market | **$24–$240/hr** | ZipRecruiter listings, Aug 2026 |
| Nonprofit graphic designer | **$26.90/hr average** ($19.95–$31.25) | ZipRecruiter, Aug 2026 |
| Design subscription — ManyPixels, dedicated designer | **from $1,299/mo** | Vendor pricing, 2026 |
| Design subscription — Design Pickle | **~$1,918/mo minimum** + $119–299/mo platform fee | Vendor pricing, 2026 |
| Design subscription — Superside (enterprise) | **~$5,000/mo** | Vendor pricing, 2026 |

**What this tells you:**
- A salaried in-house designer costs an employer roughly **$6,500–$8,800/month** all-in before overhead. Any retainer you propose below that is defensible on pure economics — say so explicitly to Segment A.
- The subscription studios have set the market's mental anchor at **$1,300–$5,000/month for "a designer on tap."** Price a retainer inside that band and you are in a familiar, pre-approved-feeling range.
- Never quote hourly to Segment B agencies. They resell your time; quote **per-slide or per-deck** so their margin maths is easy.

## 7. Qualifying — who to drop fast

Drop a prospect and move on if:
- They ask for spec work (a free sample deck for *their* actual project) before any call.
- They want "unlimited revisions" without a scope document.
- The budget conversation gets deferred twice.
- **Segment C only:** they raised pre-seed under ~$500k. There's usually no design budget yet — put them on the 12-month re-trigger list instead.

## 8. Objection handling

| Objection | Response |
|---|---|
| *"We have someone in-house."* | "Perfect — I'm not trying to replace them. I'm the person they call in the week three decks are due at once." (Especially true for Segment A.) |
| *"We use Canva / an AI tool now."* | "Those are great for volume. They're weak on the one deck that decides an outcome — the board deck, the investor deck, the pitch you can't lose." |
| *"Send us your rates."* | Don't send a rate card cold. "Rates depend on slide count and whether narrative is in scope — give me the deck and deadline and I'll price it in an hour." |
| *"Too expensive."* | Anchor to the in-house cost from §6, or reduce scope, never rate. Cutting your rate teaches them your first number was fiction. |
| *"Not right now."* | "Understood. When's your next raise / conference / annual report?" — then diary it. That's a warm lead in 4 months, not a dead one. |

---

## 9. Weekly working rhythm

- **20 new prospects contacted per week** (message 1 only) — the list is 100, so five weeks of new outreach.
- **Follow-ups are not new outreach.** Budget separate time or the cadence in §4.3 silently collapses.
- **Re-verify every trigger the morning you send it.** A job posting that closed or a funding number that got restated is the fastest way to lose credibility in one sentence.
- **Log every reply, including "no."** A "no, not now" with a date attached is the most valuable row in your CRM.
